## 2. What model are we investigating?

At each Easting \\(e\\), our pointwise GAM is

\\[ \boxed{ Y_i(e)=\beta_0(e)+\beta_B(e)I(P_i=B)+f_e(N_i)+\varepsilon_i(e) } \\]

where \\(N_i\\) is Northing, \\(I(P_i=B)\\) identifies Product B, and \\(f_e\\) is a penalized spline.

The objective is to estimate the adjusted treatment coefficient \\(\beta_B(e)\\), while allowing the Northing adjustment to be flexible.

We choose the amount of smoothness using generalized cross-validation:

\\[ \boxed{ GCV_e(\lambda)= \frac{24\\,RSS_e(\lambda)} {[24-\operatorname{EDF}\_e(\lambda)]^2} } \\]

Here, \\(RSS\\) measures residual variation, while EDF measures model complexity.

- Smaller \\(\lambda\\): less penalization, typically greater spline flexibility.
- Larger \\(\lambda\\): greater penalization, less curvature.
- As \\(\lambda\to\infty\\): the Northing smooth approaches a linear function.

The essential question is whether automatic `mgcv` optimization chooses a smoothing parameter with a GCV score as low as the best alternatives we can evaluate.

# Stage 2 — `02_Manual_Full_Field`

## 5. Purpose: Construct and study GCV without relying on `mgcv`

Completed — 79,600 manual GCV evaluations

Stage 2 is our mathematical reconstruction of penalized spline regression.

Rather than use `mgcv::gam()`, we explicitly constructed the cubic B-spline basis, calculated its second-derivative penalty, and solved the penalized least-squares equations.

The model matrix was

\\[ X=[\mathbf1,\ P,\ B_c], \\]

with the smoothing penalty \\(S\\). For each smoothing parameter,

\\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}\_\lambda = (X^\top X+\lambda S)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y. \\]

The corresponding hat matrix was

\\[ H\_\lambda= X(X^\top X+\lambda S)^{-1}X^\top. \\]

We used 400 log-\\(\lambda\\) values:

\\[ \log\lambda\in[-1,23]. \\]

Across 199 Eastings, this produced 79,600 model evaluations.

# Stage 3A — `03A_MGCV_Pilot`

## 9. Purpose: Determine whether automatic `mgcv` selection finds the lowest available GCV

Completed — Six pilot Eastings

Stage 3A was our first direct test of the automatic GCV optimizer.

We used the original model specification:

```
mgcv::gam(
  Yield ~ Product +
    s(Northing, bs = "bs", k = 10, m = c(3, 2)),
  data = d,
  family = gaussian(),
  method = "GCV.Cp"
)
```

At each Easting, we ran two approaches:

- Automatic: Let `mgcv` choose its smoothing parameter.
- Fixed grid: Explicitly supply 600 different smoothing parameters and compare the GCV scores.

We selected Eastings 212, 214, 216, 218, 232, and 234 because they include our original research sites and two regions where the preferred smoothing regime changes.

### How to interpret the main output

Open `Stage3A_Auto_vs_Grid.csv`.

The important variables are:

\\[ D\_{\mathrm{opt}}(e)= GCV\_{\mathrm{auto}}(e)-GCV\_{\mathrm{grid}}(e) \\]

and

\\[ \Delta\_\beta(e)= \widehat\beta\_{B,\mathrm{auto}}(e) - \widehat\beta\_{B,\mathrm{grid}}(e). \\]

If \\(D\_{\mathrm{opt}}>0\\), a better-scoring grid candidate exists.

If \\(\Delta\_\beta\ne0\\), the different smoothing choices produce different estimated treatment coefficients.

These are separate findings. A substantial GCV gap does not necessarily imply a substantial coefficient difference.

# Stage 3B — `03B_MGCV_Automatic_Full_Field`

## 12. Purpose: Extend automatic GAM selection to all 199 Eastings

Completed — 199 automatic GAM fits

Stage 3A showed that automatic selection can return inferior evaluated solutions.

The next question was where else this might happen across the full field.

Stage 3B fitted the GAM automatically at all 199 Eastings, but did not yet evaluate the full 600-point grid at every location.

Instead, we compared each automatic fit with an exact, independently calculated linear-limit alternative.

The reason is computational and mathematical: we already know how to calculate that limiting model through ordinary least squares.

The limiting design is

\\[ X\_\infty= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf1&P&N-\bar N \end{bmatrix}. \\]

Its total EDF is 3.

We defined

\\[ \boxed{ D\_{\mathrm{linear}}(e)= GCV\_{\mathrm{auto}}(e)-GCV\_{\mathrm{linear}}(e). } \\]

If this quantity is positive beyond the numerical tolerance, the automatic model has a worse GCV score than the straight-line alternative.

# Stage 3C — `03C_MGCV_Fixed_Grid_Full_Field`

## 15. Purpose: Examine the entire evaluated GCV landscape under the original GAM specification

Completed — 119,400 fixed-parameter GAM fits

This is the culmination of our Stage 3 work.

Stage 3B compared automatic selection against only the exact linear-limit candidate.

Stage 3C expanded that comparison by evaluating the GCV criterion over 600 fixed smoothing parameters at every Easting.

\\[ \ell_j=-12+\frac{37(j-1)}{599}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,600, \\]

where

\\[ \lambda_j=e^{\ell_j}. \\]

We calculated:

\\[ GCV_e(\lambda_j), \quad \operatorname{EDF}\_e(\lambda_j), \quad \widehat\beta_B(e;\lambda_j) \\]

for every combination of Easting and grid location.

The total was

\\[ \boxed{199\times600=119,400} \\]

fixed-parameter GAM evaluations.

This provides a numerical representation of the function

\\[ G(e,\ell)=GCV_e(e^\ell) \\]

over the observed Eastings and evaluated log smoothing parameters.

# 20. What are our main findings across Stages 2 and 3?

## A. Multiple minima occur throughout the field

| Result                                               | Manual Stage 2 | Package-based Stage 3C |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------- |
| Eastings analyzed                                    | 199            | 199                    |
| Grid points per Easting                              | 400            | 600                    |
| Total GCV evaluations                                | 79,600         | 119,400                |
| Eastings with at least one detected interior minimum | 189            | 189                    |
| Eastings with multiple detected interior minima      | 27             | 28                     |

The exact counts are slightly different because the manual and `mgcv` implementations use different spline basis and penalty parameterizations.

Both analyses nevertheless show multiple GCV minima at numerous Eastings.

## B. Some automatic selections are inferior to evaluated alternatives

Stage 3C identified 16 Eastings where automatic GCV was higher than at least one tested grid candidate.

Of these, six were already detectable using the linear-limit screen in Stage 3B.

The other ten required evaluating alternative finite smoothing parameters.

## C. The consequences for Product B are not proportional to GCV improvement

Two particularly useful cases are:

| Easting | GCV optimizer gap | Automatic minus best-grid Product B |
| ------- | ----------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| 174     | +2.439            | −1.237                              |
| 374     | +10.761           | −0.014                              |

Easting 174 has a smaller GCV discrepancy but much larger coefficient sensitivity.

This suggests that understanding the GCV criterion alone will not be sufficient. We also need to understand how \\(\widehat\beta_B\\) responds to changes in \\(\lambda\\).

## D. Changes in preferred smoothing regime do not always mean optimizer failure

Near Eastings 232–234, the automatically selected smoothing regime changes from nearly linear to more flexible.

The fixed-grid results show essentially the same preference change.

Thus a substantial change in the Product B coefficient can occur even when automatic selection agrees with the lowest evaluated candidate.

This distinction is crucial for our research.

\\[ GCV'(\lambda)=0 \iff h_2(\lambda)=h_3(\lambda), \\]

One important distinction: our existing detection process is numerical. It identifies sampled local minima from changes in the GCV curve. It does not yet constitute a hypothesis test with a calibrated false-positive rate or a proof that every stationary point has been found.

## The central takeaway

Our research has progressed through this chain:

\\[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{Validated harvest-strip observations}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Constructed full-field GCV landscapes}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Detected competing smoothing candidates}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Identified inferior automatic selections}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Measured effects on the Product B coefficient}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \textbf{Next: Refine and theoretically characterize stationary points} \end{gathered} } \\]

At the end of Stage 3C, we have a substantial and reproducible empirical diagnosis. We know where automatic smoothing selection can return inferior GCV solutions and how much those choices can affect the estimated Product B coefficient.

What we have not yet established is the complete mathematical stationary-point structure or the statistical consequences for estimating the unknown treatment effect. Those are the questions for the next stages.
